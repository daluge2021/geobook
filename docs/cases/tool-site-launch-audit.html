Eight Pages, Full Schema, 102 Real Crawlers: A Tool Site Audited Against Verified Bots

On 10 November 2026 a small calculator site went live: a homepage, three calculator pages, one how-to guide, an About page, a Contact page and a privacy policy. Eight URLs, roughly 4,700 words of visible text, structured data on every page, deployed as static HTML to a CDN. No blog, no author page, no first-party crawl log.

This is the audit of that build — including a correction. The first version of this audit reported six URLs and zero JSON-LD. Both numbers were wrong, because the audit measured a build directory that had been superseded instead of the pages the URL actually returned. Section III is about that mistake, because it is the most reusable thing here: a checklist applied to the wrong artifact produces confident, wrong conclusions.

Disclosure: the site described here was built by the same author. No client work is involved, no domain is named and no link between the two sites is published, to keep this page a structural example rather than a cross-promotion. Page figures were measured from the pages served on 21 November 2026. Traffic figures come from that site's own Cloudflare zone analytics, including verifiedBotCategory, for 14–21 November 2026.

I. What Is Actually There

Word counts below are visible text with markup and scripts stripped — what a reader or an extraction model sees, not the size of the file. Structured data was read from the served HTML, not from the repository.

Two things stand out, and they point in opposite directions. The content pages are not thin: 775 to 1,238 words each, with the calculation rule stated in text next to each widget, and an FAQ block that is marked up rather than decorative. The support pages are thin: a 117-word homepage, a 172-word About page and a 103-word Contact page carry the site's trust signals, and they are the weakest text on the domain.

So the honest starting point is not "what did they forget". It is: a site can satisfy the on-page checklist and still be invisible, which is the rest of this page.

II. What the Checklist Did Buy

It is worth separating what structured data provably does from what GEO writing claims it does, because the two are not the same.

Entity resolution. WebSite and Organization are emitted sitewide with a stable @id, and the tool pages reference that same node. A parser can tell that eight URLs are one publisher, not eight unrelated tools. That is a definitional fix, cheap and unambiguous.

Typed answers. Each FAQPage lists questions that also appear as visible text. The markup and the prose say the same thing, which is the only version that survives review: FAQ markup with questions not on the page is a policy violation, not an optimisation.

Breadcrumbs as structure, not decoration. BreadcrumbList mirrors the visible trail, so the tools sit under a known parent rather than floating as roots.

One intent per URL. Percentage math, grade weighting and date math are three pages with three titles. There is no catch-all "all tools" landing page competing with them — the directory lives on the homepage and links out.

What none of that does is generate demand. This site has had FAQPage on four pages since launch, and our own crawl logs elsewhere on this site recorded no attributable traffic from FAQ markup in fifteen days of measurement. Treat markup as an extraction and entity fix. Selling it as a traffic fix is the mistake we already corrected once in our 12-week launch record, and it would be strange to make it twice.

III. The Error Worth Keeping

The first version of this audit reported six URLs, zero JSON-LD on any page, and no analytics. The served site had eight URLs, JSON-LD on all eight, and a Cloudflare Web Analytics beacon on every page.

The cause was mundane and completely avoidable: the audit read a local build directory left over from the launch day, while a later deploy had added two pages and the entire structured-data layer. The repository was not the site. Nobody had diffed the two.

Three checks would have caught it in under a minute, and they are now the first step of any page audit here:

  • Count from the served sitemap. Fetch /sitemap-index.xml, follow the child, count <loc> entries. Eight, not six.
  • Parse the served HTML for ld+json. Not a grep of the source tree — a parse of what the URL returns, with @type values listed.
  • Measure words on the served page. If a page claims 317 words and the live page has 777, the measurement is describing something else.

Every downstream conclusion inherits the error. In the first version, a whole section proposed adding schema that was already present, because the audit had reported its absence. That is the failure mode to watch for: a confident recommendation that solves a problem which does not exist is harder to spot than a wrong number, because it reads as expertise.

The most-claimed names — GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, ChatGPT-User — have zero verified requests on this site in seven days. Every request wearing those names was a scanner. The three status codes tell the same story from another angle: 7,994 responses were 404, 5,832 were 301, 1,342 were 504, and only 1,085 were 200. Most of the traffic is probing for files that do not exist: /.env, /supabase/config.toml, /api/.env.bak, /nacos/v1/cs/configs.

The transferable rule: a crawler table ranked by user agent measures what scanners prefer to impersonate, not what AI systems are reading. Scanners claim the most famous names, so UA-based analysis is biased upward precisely for the crawlers people care most about. Any GEO dashboard without verified-bot or IP-range validation is measuring the attacker's taste.

For this site the real number is 102 verified AI requests in seven days — 86 crawler, 16 search — across eight pages. About fifteen a day, under two per page per day. That is a site being discovered, slowly, by machines that are genuinely allowed to be there. It is a start, not a result.

V. The Support Layer Is the Weak Part

With schema, word count and delivery already in place, the remaining deficits are all about who is behind the site. These are verifiable from the pages themselves:

SignalCurrent stateWhy it matters
Author identityThe guide's Article.author points at the organisation node. No named person, no author page.Entity-level trust attaches to people. An organisation-only byline is the weakest form of attribution.
Page freshnessdatePublished and dateModified are both 2026-11-10 on the guideNo revision history means no evidence the content is maintained
Homepage text117 wordsThe most-linked page has the least text; the directory is the entire body
About / Contact172 / 103 wordsThese two pages are where a reviewer checks whether a real operator exists
First-party crawl logNone — only aggregate zone analyticsPer-bot, per-page, per-day history is what makes a claim like "AI crawlers are reading us" checkable next month

Note the shape of this list: nothing here is a markup problem. The technical layer is complete and the trust layer is thin, which is the opposite of the usual tool-site pattern and the reason the first audit's conclusion was so wrong.

One operational item belongs here too. Of the 1,342 timeouts in the week, the top paths include real pages, not only scan targets, and they cluster in the same hour as the 404 peak. Scan volume can push a small origin into timeouts, and a page that times out is a page a real crawler will retry later or drop. A WAF rule on known probe paths is a crawl-budget fix, not just housekeeping.

VI. The Test: What Would Tell Us This Worked

Thresholds are written in advance, in terms spoofing cannot fake, so later claims cannot be adjusted to fit whatever happened.

CheckpointSignal (verified only)What a miss would mean
Day 30All eight URLs indexed; at least one verified AI-crawler request on a tool page rather than the homepageDiscovery is the constraint, and the fix is inbound references, not more markup
Day 60Verified AI crawls above 100/week returning 200; one tool page fetched by two different AI crawlersIf crawls rise and pages stay unquoted, the pages are thin relative to competitors
Day 90One engine, asked by hand about a convention question — which month-count rule a date calculator uses — names the pageCrawling without citation means the gap is authority, not on-page edits

Two of those rows are deliberately unflattering. A site can pass indexing and fail discovery; it can pass discovery and fail citation. Collapsing them into "traffic is up" is how build logs turn into marketing.

VII. What This Audit Cannot Prove

It is eleven days, eight pages, one operator, no control group. Page figures are inventory, not performance. Traffic figures cover one week and one zone; Cloudflare's verifier is good but not omniscient, and the 16,092 unverified requests are a mix of humans, scanners and unknown agents rather than a single category. Nothing here isolates a cause — static delivery, one-intent URLs, stated methods and complete schema all arrived together, and a second site would be needed to separate them.

What the audit can do is falsify. It falsifies the claim that a tool page is GEO-ready because it is useful: this one is useful, fully marked up, and cited by nothing measurable yet. It falsifies the traffic story a busy log tells: 16,545 requests contained 102 real AI crawls, and the busiest "GPTBot" in the table was a scanner in Brazil. And it falsifies its own first version, which reported zero schema on a site that had JSON-LD on every page — the failure mode this page exists to document.

The cheapest next step is not another tool page. It is a named author with a page, a first-party bot log that can be checked next month, and enough text on the homepage, About and Contact pages that a reviewer can tell who is running the site. Then wait ninety days and read the result against the table above.
