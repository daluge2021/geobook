Six Pages, Two Calculators, Zero Schema — A One-Week Tool Site, Audited as a GEO Asset

On 10 November 2026 a small calculator site went live: a homepage, two tool pages, an About page, a Contact page and a privacy policy. Six URLs in total, built over a handful of evenings on a static generator, deployed to a free CDN, shipped with no blog, no author page and no structured data.

This is the audit of that build. Not a success story — twelve days is far too early for one — but a check of a claim repeated constantly in GEO writing: that tool pages are the easy win. They are easy to publish. Whether they are easy to get cited is a different question, and the first week of real measurement made the difference unusually concrete.

Disclosure: the site described here was built by the same author. No client work is involved, no domain is named and no link between the two sites is published, to keep this page a structural example rather than a cross-promotion. Traffic figures come from the site's own Cloudflare zone analytics (including verifiedBotCategory), queried on 21 November 2026 for the window 14–21 November.

I. What Actually Shipped, in Numbers

Technically the build is clean: static HTML output, one inline style block per page, no client-side framework, robots.txt allowing everything and pointing at a six-URL sitemap, unique title and meta description on every page. The homepage HTML is about 7 KB. Nothing about the delivery layer is a liability.

What is missing is on the machine-readability layer, and — as the next three sections show — on the measurement layer, which turned out to matter more than expected.

II. Four Decisions That Were Right

One intent per URL. Percentage math and date math are separate pages with separate titles. There is no "all tools" landing page competing with them for the same query — the directory lives on the homepage and links out. When a site has three URLs that could answer the same question, engines pick one and the other two become noise.

The method is written next to the tool. The percentage page does not only return a number; it states the rule: convert the percent to a decimal and multiply, so 15% of 200 is 0.15 × 200 = 30. That sentence is the extractable unit. A widget alone gives an engine nothing to quote — the explanation is the part that can be attributed.

Edge cases are stated, not hidden. The date page declares that month and year breakdowns account for varying month lengths and leap years, and that reversed inputs are swapped rather than rejected. Rules written in text are checkable; behaviour buried in a script is not. This is also the only part of a calculator page that a competing engine cannot reproduce from first principles in one line.

No framework hydration. The answer text exists in the served HTML. An engine that fetches without executing JavaScript sees the same explanation a browser does. This is a low bar that a surprising number of tool sites fail, because the explanation is often rendered client-side along with the result.

III. Three Omissions We Had Already Documented

These are not novel mistakes. All three appear in this site's own 12-week launch record, which makes repeating them the useful part of this audit.

1. No structured data at all. Zero of six pages carry JSON-LD — no SoftwareApplication, no FAQPage even where an FAQ block is visibly present, no HowTo for the hand-calculation steps. Our earlier logs showed FAQPage produced no measurable crawler traffic, so this is not framed as a traffic fix. It is an extraction and entity fix, and the benefit is plausible but unmeasured. Claiming more would repeat a mistake we already corrected once.

2. No measurement for the first eight days. The build shipped without analytics or request logging, so for over a week the question "has anything actually fetched these pages" had no answer. Measurement was added on day eight. It paid for itself within an hour, in the most uncomfortable way — see the next section.

3. No named entity behind the site. About says "we", Contact is a mail link, and there is no person, no sameAs, no author page. For arithmetic this costs less than it would for a medical or financial claim, because the calculation can be verified without trusting the author. But it removes the cheapest trust signal available, and it leaves the site with nothing to attach an entity to when an engine tries to decide whether this source is a source.

IV. Where a Tool Page Wins, and Where It Cannot

The structural advantage is real and it is about intent, not markup. A query like "percentage change from 40 to 55" has one correct answer and an exact matching page. There is no editorial angle to argue about, no freshness decay — percentage arithmetic does not expire — and repeat use is normal, which is why tool pages hold attention in a way article pages rarely do.

The hard limit is just as real. An assistant can compute 15% of 200 without any source at all. So the citable asset is never the number; it is the part of the page that explains which convention was used, how edge cases are handled, and why two reasonable calculators disagree.

What a user asksCan an engine answer without a source?What the page must contain to be cited
"What is 15% of 200?"Yes — pure arithmeticNothing. No citation is winnable here.
"How is percentage change calculated?"Yes, but conventions differThe stated formula, with one worked example
"Why do two date calculators give different month counts?"Rarely — this is a convention questionNamed month-length and leap-year handling, plus the swap rule
"Which calculator handles inclusive date ranges?"No — requires a specific tool's behaviourAn explicit statement of inclusive versus exclusive counting

Read the table as a filter. Three of these four rows are winnable, and all three are won by prose that most tool sites skip in favour of a bigger button. The page that ships only the first row is competing on a query where every engine is already correct.

V. 16,545 Requests, 102 Crawlers: What the First Week of Measurement Deleted

When analytics went in on day eight, the first week of data looked like success: a six-page site no one had heard of was receiving 16,545 requests in seven days. Broken down by user agent, the named crawlers looked busy — GPTBot, OAI-SearchBot, PerplexityBot, ClaudeBot, ChatGPT-User, Googlebot, bingbot, all present.

None of that survived three cross-checks: the country the request came from, whether Cloudflare had verified the bot against its published IP ranges, and what the request actually asked for.

Two details matter more than the totals. First, GPTBot and PerplexityBot never appear in the verified list at all — every request claiming to be them was fake. Second, the largest single source country was not Brazil: it was Belgium with 4,843 requests, ahead of Brazil's 3,833 and the United States' 1,703. The scanner cluster is not one country; it is a rotating set of proxies, and the UA is just decoration.

So the number that describes reality is not 16,545. It is 102 verified AI-crawler requests in seven days — about fifteen a day across six pages, two and a half per page per day — plus 238 search-engine crawls. That is a site being discovered, slowly, by machines that are genuinely allowed to be there. It is also, not coincidentally, consistent with the other half of the picture: search clicks in single digits and an average position near 40.

The transferable rule: a crawler table ranked by user agent measures what scanners prefer to impersonate, not what AI systems are reading. Scanners claim the most famous names, so UA-based analysis is biased upward precisely for the crawlers people care most about. Any GEO dashboard without verified-bot or IP-range validation is measuring the attacker's taste.

One operational note came out of the same data, and it is not about GEO. Of the 1,342 timeouts, the top paths include the homepage and real pages, not only scan targets. Scan volume can push a small origin into timeouts, and a page that times out is a page a real crawler will retry later or drop. That is worth a WAF rule on the known probe paths and a look at why the origin is slow under load — before it becomes a crawl-budget problem rather than a nuisance.

VI. The Test: What Would Tell Us This Worked

With verified-bot data available, the checkpoints can be written in terms that spoofing cannot fake. Thresholds are set in advance so later claims cannot be adjusted to fit whatever happened.

CheckpointSignal (verified only)What a miss would mean
Day 30All six URLs indexed; at least one verified AI-crawler request on a tool page (not the homepage)Discovery is the constraint, and the fix is inbound references, not markup
Day 60Verified AI crawls above 100/week with 200-status responses; one tool page fetched by two different AI crawlersIf crawls rise but pages stay unquoted, the pages are thin relative to competitors
Day 90One engine, asked by hand about a convention question, names the pageCrawling without citation means the gap is authority — inbound mentions, not on-page edits

Two of those rows are deliberately unflattering. A site can pass indexing and fail discovery; it can pass discovery and fail citation. Collapsing them into "traffic is up" is how build logs turn into marketing.

VII. What This Audit Cannot Prove

It is twelve days, two tools, one operator, no control group. Page figures are inventory, not performance. Traffic figures cover one week and one zone; Cloudflare's verifier is good but not omniscient, and the 16,092 unverified requests are a mix of humans, scanners and unknown agents rather than a single category. Nothing here isolates a cause: static delivery, one-intent URLs, stated methods and missing schema all arrived together, and a second site would be needed to separate them.

What the audit can do is falsify. It falsifies the idea that a tool page is automatically GEO-ready because it is useful — this one is useful and still has no structured data, no author entity and, for its first eight days, no measurement. It falsifies the traffic story that a busy log tells: 16,545 requests contained 102 real AI crawls, and the busiest "crawler" in the table was a scanner in Brazil. And it falsifies the opposite overcorrection, that schema and llms.txt are what make a tool page citable; the winnable queries in section IV are won by a paragraph of convention detail.

The cheapest next step is not another tool page. It is verified-bot measurement on every page, a named author, and one paragraph per calculator that states the rule and the exception. Then wait ninety days and read the result against the table above.
