A 12-Week GEO Launch, Documented Week by Week — This Site's Own Case

Most "full process" GEO case studies are composites: an anonymized brand, representative figures, a tidy three-month arc. This one is not. It is the record of what happened to this site between 4 August and the end of October 2026, taken from a Cloudflare Worker log that records every request, and from a revision history you can read in the repository.

It includes the week a 778-request "win" turned out to be a scanner, the infrastructure that produced zero requests, and the one measurable citation we can point to. Read it as a log, not a template: your starting point will differ, and the mistakes are the transferable part.

I. Week 0: The Baseline We Actually Had

On 4 August 2026 the site had 47 articles, Article JSON-LD with dates, an llms.txt, clean URLs and a sitemap. It also had no crawler logging, no /ai/ endpoints, no author identity beyond a generic byline, and no idea which AI systems were reading it. The first day of logging recorded 303 requests, 90 of them from identified bots.

That gap — content yes, measurement no — is the most common starting position, and it is the reason the first two weeks were spent on instrumentation rather than writing.

II. Weeks 1–2: Build the Baseline Before You Optimize

Eighteen commits across five workstreams shipped in the first week: request logging into D1, the .well-known/ files, /ai/ JSON summaries, a site-wide date normalization, and brand-name consistency across titles, Open Graph and schema.

The same week produced the least glamorous fix of the whole project. The origin had been switched to raw file fetching behind a sandbox CSP header, and scripts were failing silently: the pages looked fine, and the structured data was not being read the way we assumed. Nothing in an audit tool would have flagged it. Only comparing rendered output against source did.

III. Weeks 3–4: Publish, and Learn to Read Noise

Content weeks looked like this: a community module and two JSON-LD articles on 08-09, the content template and a security-header pass on 08-10, a new article that was crawled ten times on its launch day (08-12), and a definition moved above the fold on 08-14.

Then 08-11 arrived with 778 requests — the biggest day of the project. It was noise. 559 of those were bots probing /.env.production and similar paths, labelled ChatGPT-User and Amazonbot but behaving like a scanner. Two days later a second wave arrived: a spoofed GPTBot that our Worker blocked. The lesson cost us a day of celebration and produced a permanent rule — never count a spike until the paths and status codes have been checked.

IV. Weeks 5–8: Attribute the Traffic, Honestly

By mid-August we could answer the question every GEO vendor dodges: which interventions actually produced crawler traffic? Across 15 days (4–19 August) the ranking was almost inverted from the marketing consensus.

Two of those rows were our own mistakes. The /ai/ endpoints were built because audit tools check for them; no crawler we could see ever fetched one. FAQPage was shipped expecting a crawl effect that did not appear — it may still help at the extraction stage, but that is a different claim, and we no longer make the stronger one.

V. Weeks 9–12: The Only Result That Counts

Crawl traffic is a means, not an outcome. The outcome we could measure was citation. Over 12 days in late August the site received 4,115 crawler requests from 13 identified crawlers — Bingbot 326, ClaudeBot 254, Googlebot 216, Amazonbot 178, OAI-SearchBot 111, ChatGPT-User 61, GPTBot 45, PerplexityBot 29 — and in September we put four engines to the test: the same definitional question, asked by hand, answers recorded.

One of four engines cited this site, quoting the opening definition almost verbatim and linking back. Three said the term was not standard and named other sources or none. That is the honest endpoint of twelve weeks: retrievable, occasionally quotable, not authoritative.

If we started again, three changes. Instrument before publishing: the first two weeks of logs are worth more than the first two weeks of articles, because they make every later claim checkable. Do not build discovery files expecting traffic; build them because they are cheap and correct, and measure them rather than assume. And start third-party corroboration on day one — being named by someone else is the variable we never moved, and it is the one that separates "retrievable" from "cited".

VII. What This Case Cannot Prove

It is one site, one topic, one author, twelve weeks. There is no control group: new articles, schema changes, date fixes and security headers all shipped in the same window, so no row in the tables above isolates a single cause. Crawler identity comes from user agents, which are spoofable — that is how the 778-request day happened. And the citation result is one query on one date with no repeated samples.

What it can do is falsify things. It falsifies "add llms.txt and AI will find you". It falsifies "FAQ schema increases AI crawler traffic". It falsifies the idea that a traffic spike is evidence of progress. Those are worth more than another success story, because you can check them against your own logs this week.
