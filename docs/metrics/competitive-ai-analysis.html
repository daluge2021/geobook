How to Benchmark Competitors in AI Answers

Your own AI visibility is half the picture. The other half is whether your three rivals beat you in the answer engines your buyers use — and where, exactly, the gap is.
Competitive AI analysis is not a secret dashboard. It is a disciplined mirror of your own benchmarking: a fixed query set, a fixed scoring rubric, a fixed cadence. This article gives you the method you can start with a spreadsheet and a browser today.

Why It Works Like Your Own Benchmarking — Only Wider

Everything that makes GEO benchmarking honest applies to competitors, with one addition: you now score more than one brand per answer. The core discipline stays the same — fixed queries, fixed formula, monthly cadence — because AI answers drift substantially month to month. A snapshot is noise; a four-month trend is a signal.

Also keep the same honesty rules: engine-by-engine comparison only (never average ChatGPT against Perplexity), and always score every competitor on the same answers you award yourself, never different sessions.

Step 1: Fix the Query Set

Your competitive set is only as good as your question list. Build 20-50 questions from three buckets:

  • Revenue-critical — the questions that genuinely move your pipeline. Where AI is asked instead of Google, this is where you need to beat everyone.
  • Category-owning — the questions where a citation effectively = "the answer is this brand."
  • Comparison — "best / vs / alternatives" queries, where community sources pull heavy weight.

Write the questions exactly as a buyer would type them. Save them verbatim, and mark which 3-5 underlie actual deals — that weighting is what separates a leaderboard from a vanity board.

Step 2: Choose the Competitor Set

Track 4-6 names: 3-4 real rivals on your core questions, plus 1-2 benchmark targets you do not conventionally compete with as much (a category leader, or a fast-moving newcomer). Scoring an aspirational target is how you find the gap between your ceiling and theirs. Refer to source matrix logic for why some competitors win from platform sources you are ignoring.

Step 3: The Presence Scale

Score each competitor per question, per engine, on a simple 0-3 scale. This mirrors the "absent / mention / cite / recommend" reality of answers:

ScorePresenceExample
0Not presentBrand not named in the answer
1MentionedListed among options without a source link
2CitedNamed with an explicit source reference
3RecommendedPresented as the primary or recommended choice

Apply the same rubric every cycle. The scale is directional on purpose — fine-grained sentiment scoring invites false precision. The job is to find cite-level and recommend-level gaps that you can act on, not to argue about tenths of a point.

Step 4: Score, Aggregate, and Read the Leaderboard

Run the set in 2-3 engines on the same day. Aggregate per competitor into three numbers: raw presence score (sum of the scale), percent of answers where the brand appears at all (a share-like figure comparable to citation share), and recommend/cite counts on the revenue-critical subset.

A compact read:

CompetitorPresence % (all queries)Revenue-critical cite/recommendRead
You28%6 of 20Solid presence, thin on money queries
Rival A44%13 of 20Leader — dominates this query library
Rival B19%4 of 20Contender — competitive on a few topics
Benchmark target61%17 of 20Reference ceiling — study its sources

Against vendor-published norms, sustained overall presence around 15%+ is typically considered a leadership-ish position and 40%+ clearly so — but the only honest yardstick is your gap to the next competitor, recomputed monthly.

Step 5: Diagnose Where Rivals Win From

The leaderboard says who; the diagnosis says why. For each revenue-critical answer where a competitor beats you, open the cited/linked sources and bucket them:

  • Competitor's own site (owned) — they have a stronger fact base; build the missing answer assets.
  • Media/reports (earned) — they have independent witnesses; seed your own data and press.
  • Community platforms (platform) — they are winning the experience conversation; publish where engines actually quote.

This mirrors attribution logic: know which source type carries the win before you spend against it.

Step 6: Turn Findings into Objectives

Close the loop with measurable targets:

  1. Pick the 5 revenue-critical questions where a rival holds a cite or recommend level you lack.
  2. Build the specific answer asset, schema, or third-party source that closes one of those gaps.
  3. Re-run the set monthly and watch whether the gap narrows. Congratulate the team only on a trend that holds two cycles.

Feed the result into your GEO dashboard alongside core metrics so competitive position is part of the standing report, not a one-off exercise.

Automation Notes (and When to Stay Manual)

Start manual — a spreadsheet and 30-60 minutes per cycle is enough to learn the rhythm. Tools that offer competitive AI tracking can scale the query library and engine count once your rubric is locked, but do not let a tool redefine your scoring scale mid-program; continuity beats tool fidelity. Also keep double-blind checks early on (two people scoring the same answers) to confirm the rubric is shared.

FAQ

What is the quickest way to benchmark competitors in AI answers? Manually, weekly: 20-50 fixed questions across 2-3 engines, scoring each competitor 0-3 on the same presence scale. Consistency beats tooling.

How do I score competitor presence? 0 = absent, 1 = mentioned, 2 = cited, 3 = recommended; weight the revenue-critical subset. Apply the same rubric every cycle and track trends, not point values.

How often should I run competitive AI analysis? Weekly for priority queries, monthly for the wider library. AI answers drift month to month, so compare trends across four or more cycles.

How do I translate the leaderboard into action? Find where rivals beat you at cite/recommend level on revenue questions, bucket their sources (owned / earned / platform), and allocate effort to the biggest gap.
